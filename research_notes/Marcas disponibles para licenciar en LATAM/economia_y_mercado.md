# Apparel/footwear brand licensing and distribution in LATAM: deal economics, contract terms and market context (as of Nov 2026)

Research date: 26 Nov 2026. Notation: **[HARD]** = company filing, official or government data; **[EST]** = market-research estimate or modelled forecast; **[OLD]** = source dated before 2025, may be outdated. Search was in English, Spanish and Portuguese. Several key sources (Licensing International full study, Euromonitor country reports) are paywalled, so only their public summaries were used.

---

## 1. What royalty %, minimum guarantees and marketing contributions are typical for sportswear/apparel licenses in LATAM, and how do they compare to global benchmarks?

### Takeaway
I found no public LATAM-specific royalty benchmark. The best public benchmarks are North American: licensed apparel averages about 9.7% royalty, footwear about 8%, and the all-category average about 8.9%. Common practice quotes a 5–15% range on wholesale/net sales. All of this data is from 2018–2020 or older. Deals pair the royalty with a minimum guarantee (MG), often partly prepaid as an advance credited against royalties, and a separate advertising/marketing contribution set as a % of net sales. Licensing in LATAM is growing faster than the global average: +7.6% in 2025 against +5.45% globally.

### Cited Findings
**Global royalty benchmarks (all [OLD])**
- In The Licensing Letter's 2019–2020 survey, licensed apparel averaged a 9.71% royalty, flat on the prior year. Apparel was 9.50% in 2014 and 9.66% in 2017. — [The Licensing Letter, "Royalty rates flat"](https://www.thelicensingletter.com/royalty-rates-flat/) (c. 2020) [OLD]
- Footwear averaged 8.02% in 2018 (down 0.8%) and accessories 9.05% in 2018. — [The Licensing Letter](https://www.thelicensingletter.com/?p=1831) (c. 2019) [OLD]
- Apparel-based *properties* (brands whose core is apparel) commanded an average of 8.41%, with a range of **5–17%**. This is a property-type metric, not a product-category metric. — [The Licensing Letter](https://www.thelicensingletter.com/?p=1827) (c. 2019–2020) [OLD]
- The industry-wide average royalty in the US and Canada was 8.87% in 2019, up only 0.3 points since 2010. — [The Licensing Letter, "Average industry-wide royalty rate up 0.7 points to 8.87% in 2019"](https://www.thelicensingletter.com/average-industry-wide-royalty-rate-up-0-7-percentage-points-to-reach-8-87-in-2019/) (2020) [OLD]
- A sports-licensing agency states that royalties usually sit between **5% and 15% of wholesale sales**. A licensing project typically combines an MG (described as an "insurance policy" for the licensor) with a variable royalty on sales. — [RTR Sports, "Brand licensing: cost review"](https://rtrsports.com/es/blog/marca-licencia-coste-revision-dinero-detras-del-proceso) (undated agency blog; not a survey)

**MG and advance structure (illustrative contract data points, [OLD])**
- In an apparel license filed with the SEC, the licensee agreed to an **8% royalty on net sales**, an MG of **US$750,000**, and an advance of **US$187,500 (25% of the MG)** due at signing. — [Biscayne Apparel 10-Q, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000088706/000095014497012192/0000950144-97-012192.txt/seq-2) (1997) [OLD]
- Another SEC-filed license has a **US$40,000 MG with a US$20,000 non-refundable advance** credited against later royalties. — [Genius Products 10-KSB, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001098016/000101968701000573/0004.txt) (2000) [OLD]
- In a third SEC-filed license, the advance is credited against that year's guaranteed minimum. — [Fashion House Holdings 10-KSB, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000797329/000095014806000024/v19173kexv10w8.htm) (2005) [OLD]
- Licensors often require the MG to be paid in advance or on a defined schedule. The parties must define the royalty base (gross vs. net), the allowed deductions (returns, promotional allowances, taxes), and how statements are reported (by category or SKU). — [Morgan Lewis, "Merchandise License Agreements"](https://www.morganlewis.com/zh-tw/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2022/02/merchandise-license-agreements) (Feb 2022) [OLD]

**Marketing contributions**
- Advertising obligations in apparel/accessory licenses are set as a **% of net sales** and are separate from the royalty. One watch/accessory license has both a co-op marketing percentage and an additional "Image Fund". The exact % could not be extracted from the search output. — [G-III Apparel 10-Q exhibit, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000821002/000095013604004407/file002.htm) (2004) [OLD]; [Movado Group 10-Q exhibit, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000072573/000119312511239025/dex101.htm) (2011) [OLD]

**LATAM licensing market size and growth (current)**
- Global licensed merchandise and services sales reached **US$389.8bn in 2025 (+5.45%)**. **Latin America grew +7.6%**, the second-fastest region after North Asia (+14.1%). The study draws on 1,068 companies and 51 countries. Regional and country breakdowns are only in the paid full report. — [Licensing International 2026 Global Study press release](https://licensinginternational.org/news/licensing-internationals-2026-global-study-shows-licensing-industrys-continued-growth-reaching-389-8-billion-in-sales/) (May 2026) [HARD, survey-based]; [License Global](https://www.licenseglobal.com/trends-insights/global-licensing-industry-reaches-389-8-billion-with-5-45-growth) (May 2026)
- 2024 baseline: US$369.6bn globally (+3.7% on 2023). **Apparel remained the largest product category.** — [Licensing International 2025 study](https://licensinginternational.org/news/licensing-internationals-2025-global-study-shows-licensing-industry-reached-369-6-billion/) (May 2025)
- License Global's 2026 agents report puts Latin America at **US$11.1bn in retail sales of licensed consumer products handled by licensing agents in 2025**. This is a different measure from the Licensing International total. — [License Global via AOL](https://www.lite.aol.com/entertainment/story/0022/20260402/9682910.htm) (Apr 2026)
- The full Licensing International PDF costs US$5,500 for non-members. Royalty summary tables exist in it but are not public. — [LI 2024 Global Licensing Industry Study (PDF)](https://spielwarenmesse.de/fileadmin/SWM_Dateien/PDF/Allgemein/Lizenzen/LI_2024_Global_Licensing_Industry_Study.pdf) (2024) [OLD]

**Withholding tax and remittance on royalties (affects the licensee's all-in cost)**
- In **Brazil**, deducting trademark royalties paid abroad has historically required registering the contract with **INPI** (and the Central Bank) and staying within statutory limits. These include a **5% cap** for related-party royalties. — [International Tax Review](https://www.internationaltaxreview.com/article/b1f7n13nbrmd34/the-deductibility-of-royalties-paid-abroad-the-new-environment-in-brazil) (c. 2017–2018) [OLD]
- Since 1 July 2017 (INPI Normative Instruction 70/2017), INPI registers license contracts without checking whether the royalty % meets tax parameters. — [International Tax Review](https://www.internationaltaxreview.com/article/b1f7n13nbrmd34/the-deductibility-of-royalties-paid-abroad-the-new-environment-in-brazil) [OLD]; [WIPO, Brazil comments](https://www.wipo.int/export/sites/www/scp/en/meetings/session_30/comments_received/brazil_4.pdf) [OLD]
- In a 2018 ruling (Brazil–France treaty), Receita Federal applied **15% withholding tax plus 10% CIDE** to trademark-use payments to a French licensor. Brazil and the US have no double-tax treaty. — [International Tax Review](https://www.internationaltaxreview.com/article/2a68vuj6k5124dswziy2o/brazil-payments-for-trademark-use-to-french-entity-subject-to-withholding-tax) (2018) [OLD]

### Inferences
- If a LATAM sportswear license is quoted at 8–12% of net wholesale sales, it sits inside the global apparel range (avg ~9.7%, range 5–17%). Quotes above ~12% would be at the high end unless the brand is a top-tier global property. This is inferred from dated North American data, so it should be checked against current LATAM deal comps.
- The 1997 SEC example (advance = 25% of the first-year MG) is consistent with the "MG paid partly in advance and credited against royalties" structure that the Morgan Lewis and RTR sources describe. The specific % is a single old data point, not a benchmark.
- In Brazil, withholding tax plus CIDE (and other taxes not covered here) add a significant tax layer on top of the headline royalty. The licensee should model royalty cost "grossed up", and contracts should say who bears these taxes.
- LATAM licensing growth (+7.6% in 2025) outpacing global (+5.45%) suggests licensors are actively seeking LATAM partners. That gives licensees some negotiating leverage on MGs for second-tier brands.

### Gaps
- **No public LATAM-specific royalty, MG, advance or marketing-fund benchmarks were found.** Licensing International's regional royalty tables are paywalled. The Licensing Letter's latest public apparel figure is from the 2019–2020 survey.
- **Typical marketing/advertising fund %:** No source found with a current figure. Industry practice is often described informally as roughly 1–5% of net sales, split between a licensor "brand fund" and local licensee spend. This was **not verified** in this research and should be confirmed with a licensing agent or counsel.
- **Typical MG as a % of projected royalties** (e.g., MG set at 60–80% of forecast royalties) was not verified by any source.
- **Licensor sourcing-center models** (licensee must buy through the licensor's sourcing offices or agents, paying a buying commission of roughly 5–10% FOB on top of the royalty) were not documented in any source found. This needs direct inquiry with the licensor or counsel.
- Current Brazil withholding/CIDE/PIS-COFINS rates on royalties, and the 2026 status of the tax reform's effect on royalty imports, were not confirmed. Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, Chile and Peru royalty withholding rates were not researched.

## 2. What contract clauses matter most for a licensee already holding a sportswear license (category exclusivity, competing-brand restrictions, change-of-control)?

### Takeaway
Public sources cover the standard clause set: grant scope, term and sell-off, territory and channels, category/geographic exclusivity, royalty base, MG, approvals, and audit. They give almost nothing on non-compete or competing-brand clauses or on change of control. The LATAM deal examples show that sportswear licenses are often bundled with distribution, franchise and import contracts, with long terms (e.g., 10 years). For a licensee holding an existing sportswear license, the critical clauses are any non-compete in the existing license, the category definitions in the new one, and channel/e-commerce carve-outs. These are not covered by public sources.

### Cited Findings
- The grant should specify the licensed IP, the permitted uses (develop, manufacture, distribute, promote, sell), packaging and marketing materials, **territory and channels** (e-commerce, retail, wholesale, distributors), and **exclusivity by product category or geography**. A defined term plus a **post-term sell-off period** is needed for inventory. — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/zh-tw/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2022/02/merchandise-license-agreements) (Feb 2022) [OLD]
- Licensors require approvals at several stages (concept, artwork, pre-production samples, production samples, advertising), so the licensee should negotiate approval timelines. Audit rights are a major cost issue, and licensees should limit their frequency, duration and scope. Underpayment remedies can include termination and reimbursement of audit costs. — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/zh-tw/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2022/02/merchandise-license-agreements) [OLD]
- INTA publishes a Brand License Template Checklist (Aug 2024) that can be used as a clause checklist. Its content was not reviewed in this research. — [INTA COBC Brand License Template Checklist (PDF)](https://inta.org/wp-content/uploads/public-files/advocacy/committee-reports/COBC-Brand-License-Template-Checklist_8_26_24.pdf) (Aug 2024) [OLD, not reviewed]
- **Deal example, Brazil:** In 2018 Vulcabras became the **exclusive distributor and licensee of Under Armour in Brazil for footwear, apparel and accessories for 10 years**. It acquired UA Brasil for R$97.5m (paid in nine monthly instalments). Franchise and import contracts were signed with Under Armour group companies alongside. — [Mercado & Consumo](https://mercadoeconsumo.com.br/02/10/2018/noticias/vulcabras-azaleia-e-under-armour-firmam-acordo-de-distribuicao-e-licenciamento/) (Oct 2018) [OLD]; [Fusões & Aquisições](https://fusoesaquisicoes.com/acontece-no-setor/vulcabras-azaleia-conclui-aquisicao-da/) [OLD]
- In March 2026 Vulcabras was described as representing Under Armour (and Mizuno) in Brazil under long-term contracts that allow local development and **manufacture** of models adapted to the Brazilian market. The company was resuming its search for new brand partners. — [Mercado & Consumo](https://mercadoeconsumo.com.br/09/03/2026/economia/vulcabras-retoma-busca-parceiros-ampliar-negocios/) (9 Mar 2026); [Times Brasil/CNBC](https://timesbrasil.com.br/brasil/vulcabras-retoma-neste-ano-busca-por-parceiros-para-ampliar-negocio/) (2026)
- Vulcabras, owner of the local brand Olympikus, holds both Under Armour and Mizuno in Brazil. This shows that one licensee can hold multiple competing sportswear brands, a local brand included, when the licensors accept it. — [NeoFeed](https://neofeed.com.br/negocios/dona-da-olympikus-e-da-under-armour-agora-corre-tambem-com-mizuno-no-brasil) (date not confirmed; c. 2024–2025)
- **Deal example, Authentic Brands Group (Reebok):** ABG works in LATAM through many category- and country-specific licensees rather than one master licensee. In Brazil it has more than 14 brands and more than 24 partners (e.g., Save Comercial e Importadora for Reebok, Dilly Sports for Reebok kids footwear, RobSol for Reebok eyewear). In Mexico it has 25 brands through 45 licensees and commercial partners, managed from a Mexico City (Polanco) hub. — [SGI Europe](https://www.sgieurope.com/people/authentic-opens-mexico-office/117785.article) (date c. 2025); [Licensing International, Dilly Sports](https://licensinginternational.org/news/authentic-brands-group-and-dilly-sports-announce-strategic-partnership-to-expand-reebok-kids-footwear-in-brazil/) (c. 2025); [FashionUnited, RobSol eyewear](https://fashionunited.com/news/fashion/reebok-signs-long-term-deal-for-eyewear-in-brazil/2025112269325) (Nov 2025); [Modaes](https://www.modaes.com/global/companies/authentic-brands-more-in-mexico-latin-american-headquarters-to-double-business-in-five-years) (c. 2025)
- ABG also signed a region-wide e-commerce partnership with **Mercado Libre** (Reebok, Champion, Aéropostale, Nine West, Nautica, Dockers). This affects how channels are divided between licensees and the licensor's own marketplace deals. — [SGB Online](https://sgbonline.com/authentic-brands-group-partners-with-latin-americas-mercado-libre/) (date c. 2024–2025)
- Reebok Mexico's general manager said that **ABG's backing helped mitigate cost increases from the extra tariffs on non-FTA countries such as China**. This suggests the licensor's sourcing and pricing support matters in a tariff shock. — [Expansión](https://expansion.mx/empresas/2026/01/08/reebok-revela-estrategia-crecer-en-mexico) (8 Jan 2026)

### Inferences
- A licensee already holding a sportswear license should check its existing contract for (a) non-compete clauses covering "competing brands" or "athletic/sport" categories, (b) key-account or channel exclusivities, and (c) consent requirements before taking on another brand. The Vulcabras case (UA + Mizuno + Olympikus) shows that holding several sportswear brands at once is negotiable.
- Because licensors like ABG divide rights by category, country and channel, and run their own marketplace deals (Mercado Libre), new licenses should define precisely: product categories (performance vs. lifestyle, kids, accessories), countries (including Central America and the Caribbean individually), and e-commerce rights (own site, marketplaces, cross-border). This prevents channel conflict.
- Long LATAM terms (10 years in the UA–Vulcabras case) often come with acquisition of the licensor's local operation, franchise and import agreements, and local manufacturing rights. A licensee should negotiate renewal options and exit/termination compensation (inventory buy-back, sell-off) in proportion to that investment.
- A tariff-pass-through or "material adverse change" mechanism (e.g., MG relief when import duties rise sharply) is worth negotiating, given Mexico's 2024–2026 tariff hikes and the Reebok comment. This is an inference; no source documents such clauses.

### Gaps
- No public source covered typical **change-of-control**, **assignment**, **sublicensing** or **non-compete/competing-brand** clauses in LATAM sportswear licenses, or how they are negotiated.
- No legal-firm guides specific to licensing in Mexico, Brazil, Colombia, Argentina or Chile were reviewed. Points such as IMPI registration effects in Mexico, INPI registration in Brazil, and distributor indemnity on termination under local law (e.g., agency/distribution indemnities in Colombia and Chile) need counsel input.
- The financial terms of the UA–Vulcabras and Reebok LATAM deals (royalty %, MG) are not public.

## 3. What are the size and growth rates of the sportswear market by major LATAM country, and which segments are growing fastest in 2025–2026?

### Takeaway
Third-party market-size estimates for LATAM sportswear disagree by an order of magnitude (from US$0.7bn to US$20bn for "LATAM"), so treat them as low-confidence. Brazil (~US$4.4bn sportswear 2025, IMARC) and Mexico (~US$1.5–2.0bn sports apparel, Grand View) are the largest markets. Euromonitor confirms that Brazil and Mexico are the biggest markets for all top-10 players. Hard data from brands shows strong momentum: adidas LATAM grew +21% currency-neutral in 2025 and +16% c.n. in Q3 2026, led by **running, football, training and Originals/lifestyle**. Running is the clearest boom segment, especially in Brazil and Colombia.

### Cited Findings
**Hard company data**
- **adidas Latin America 2025:** net sales **€2,926m** (+6% in euros; **+21% currency-neutral**). Gross margin 47.5%, operating margin 20.8%. Drivers were **double-digit growth in Running, Football and Training (Performance) and in Originals and Sportswear (Lifestyle)**. — [adidas Annual Report 2025, Latin America segment](https://report.adidas-group.com/2025/en/group-management-report-financial-review/business-performance-by-segment/latin-america.html) (Mar 2026) [HARD]
- **adidas Q2 2026:** LATAM +28% currency-neutral, the company's fastest-growing market, led by Performance, particularly **football and running**. — summarized in search results from [Merca2.0](https://www.merca20.com/?p=12609523) (2026); figure not independently confirmed from the adidas release.
- **adidas Q3 2026:** LATAM revenue **€928m, +16% currency-neutral, +29% reported**. Group apparel grew ~19% c.n. while footwear was roughly flat. Football grew 13%. — [Exchange4media](https://www.exchange4media.com/marketing-news/adidas-q3-revenue-hits-record-eur725-billion-marketing-spend-rises-11-160476.html) (Oct/Nov 2026); [World Footwear](https://www.worldfootwear.com/news/adidas-reports-strong-third-quarter-results/11874.html) (2026)
- **Reebok Mexico:** 16 own stores (the first opened in Aug 2023), with 3–4 openings planned for 2026. Sales **doubled** after two years of double-digit growth, the 2026 growth target is ~40%, and market share is **4–4.5%**. Priorities are training/cross-training and running, plus the 11–15 age segment (rising from 3% to ~15% of the business). It plans to double its presence in Martí and Innovasport. — [Expansión](https://expansion.mx/empresas/2026/01/08/reebok-revela-estrategia-crecer-en-mexico) (8 Jan 2026) [HARD, company statements]
- **Colombia:** Skechers reached a **16.58% share in 2025**, becoming the second-strongest sports brand in the country, with emphasis on running. — [Valora Analitik](https://www.valoraanalitik.com/?p=615282) (2026)
- **Colombia textile/apparel sector (MinCIT):** production +7.5% and sales +1.8% (to Sept 2025). Exports were US$517m (Jan–Oct 2025). Textile-apparel, leather and footwear together make up about 10% of manufacturing GDP. — [MinCIT](https://www.mincit.gov.co/prensa/noticias/industria/medidas-en-beneficio-industria-textil-confecciones) (29 Jan 2026) [HARD]
- **Shein was the third-largest apparel and footwear player in Latin America in 2025**, with strong positions in Brazil, Chile and Mexico. — [Euromonitor, "Chinese brands increase competition in Latin American fashion"](https://www.euromonitor.com/article/chinese-brands-increase-competition-in-latin-american-fashion) (16 Nov 2026)
- Euromonitor: Brazil and Mexico are the biggest markets for all top-10 apparel/footwear players in Latin America. — [Euromonitor, Apparel and Footwear in Latin America](https://www.euromonitor.com/apparel-and-footwear-in-latin-america/report) (Mar 2025)

**Trend signals 2025–2026**
- **Running boom, Colombia:** brands such as Under Armour (Velociti family launch, "Speed Session" events, an elite team) are investing in response to rising race participation. — [Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/tendencias/sociales/el-boom-del-running-en-colombia-activa-la-inversion-de-marcas-internacionales-en-indumentaria-deportiva-492202) (17 Apr 2026)

- **Running, Brazil:** local brand Olympikus is gaining share in running. — [The Brazilian Report newsletter](https://newsletters.brazilian.report/p/running-shoes-olympikus) (c. 2025)
- **Football/retro:** adidas reports double-digit growth in Football and Originals in LATAM in 2025, and football again led Q2 2026, a FIFA World Cup year. — [adidas AR 2025](https://report.adidas-group.com/2025/en/group-management-report-financial-review/business-performance-by-segment/latin-america.html). Reebok sponsors Panama's national team for the World Cup. — [Expansión](https://expansion.mx/empresas/2026/01/08/reebok-revela-estrategia-crecer-en-mexico) (Jan 2026)

### Inferences
- Brand-level growth (adidas LATAM +21% c.n. in 2025, Reebok Mexico doubling) is far above the 3–5% CAGRs in market-research reports. Either the large brands are taking share from local/unbranded players, or the reports understate nominal (inflation-driven) growth, especially in Argentina. Licensees should build business plans on brand-specific sell-through data, not on generic CAGRs.
- The fastest-growing segments in 2025–2026 are **running (footwear and apparel)**, **football (World Cup 2026 year, jerseys and retro/Originals lifestyle)** and **training**. A new license with credible running or football heritage fits current demand. Lifestyle retro silhouettes (e.g., Reebok Club C) are being de-emphasized by at least one brand in Mexico in favor of performance.
- Argentina-specific figures were not found. Euromonitor-level data indicates the core markets are Brazil and Mexico, then Colombia, Chile, Argentina and Peru.

### Gaps
- No reliable, comparable 2025 sportswear market sizes were found for **Argentina, Chile, Peru, Colombia (apparel) or Central America**. The Euromonitor country reports are paywalled, and lp.euromonitor.com could not be reached from this session (blocked by network settings).
- The figure of 13 million street runners in Brazil (attributed to the Brazilian Street Running Association) and 5,241 races in 2025 vs. 2,827 in 2024 appeared only in search summaries (one from LinkedIn) and was **not verified**.
- No source quantified the "retro football / terrace culture" trend at country level (e.g., Samba/Gazelle sales in Argentina or Mexico).
- No Circana/NPD LATAM sportswear data was found publicly.

## 4. Which trade/tariff changes in 2024–2026 make local manufacturing vs. importing more or less attractive by country?

### Takeaway
Policy diverged sharply. **Mexico** and **Colombia** raised barriers on non-FTA (mainly Chinese) apparel and footwear. Mexico applies 35% on apparel and up to 35% on footwear for non-FTA origins from 2026, plus 33.5% on courier parcels. Colombia keeps 40% on apparel from non-FTA countries while zeroing input duties. Both favor local or FTA-origin production. **Argentina** cut apparel and footwear tariffs from 35% to 20% in 2025 and dismantled non-tariff barriers, which favors importing. **Brazil** zeroed the federal import tax on cross-border B2C parcels under US$50 in May 2026 (more Shein/Temu pressure) but keeps antidumping duties on Chinese footwear, which pushes sourcing to Indonesia and elsewhere. **Chile** removed the VAT exemption on small foreign purchases in Oct 2025, levelling the field for local retail.

**Chile**
- From **25 Oct 2025** (Ley de Cumplimiento Tributario, 2024), foreign platforms (AliExpress, Shein, Amazon, Temu and others) must charge **19% VAT on purchases up to US$500**, ending the US$41 VAT exemption. **Peru**
- China supplied **57% of Peru's apparel imports in 2025**. — [Euromonitor](https://www.euromonitor.com/article/chinese-brands-increase-competition-in-latin-american-fashion) (Nov 2026)

### Inferences
- **Mexico:** 35% duties on non-FTA apparel and footwear (Dec 2024 and Jan 2026 decrees) plus IMMEX restrictions raise landed costs for China-sourced licensed product. That makes **local production or sourcing from FTA partners** relatively more attractive. Candidates are USMCA partners, Central America, Colombia, and CPTPP members such as Vietnam, subject to rules of origin, which were not verified here. For a licensee, a licensor's mandatory Asian sourcing center can become a cost disadvantage in Mexico unless origin is FTA-eligible. The 33.5% courier rate also reduces Shein/Temu price pressure relative to 2024.
- **Colombia:** 40% on non-FTA finished apparel combined with 0% on inputs and yarns strongly favors **local manufacturing (cut-make-trim) of licensed apparel**, or sourcing from FTA partners (e.g., Mexico, Peru, Central America, the US, the EU).
- **Argentina:** duty cuts (35% to 20% on apparel and footwear) and the removal of non-tariff barriers make **importing finished licensed product** more viable than in 2023. Local manufacturing is now under heavy competitive pressure, according to industry groups.
- **Brazil:** the zero federal tax on sub-US$50 cross-border purchases intensifies competition at the low end of apparel. Finished footwear from China still faces antidumping, so licensed footwear should be sourced from non-Chinese Asia or made locally, as Vulcabras does with UA and Mizuno. Brazil's larger scale and local footwear industry make local production of licensed footwear a proven model.
- **Chile:** VAT parity on cross-border purchases (Oct 2025) slightly improves the position of local licensed retail. Chile remains the most import-dependent and China-dependent apparel market (71%).

### Gaps
- No exact per-line rates were obtained for Mexico's chapters 61, 62 and 64 under the Jan 2026 and Apr 2026 decrees, nor whether the Dec 2024 temporary apparel decree was extended or absorbed. Footwear 25% vs. 35% is unresolved (sources conflict).
- No 2026 Argentine tariff changes for apparel or footwear were found (the 2025 decree is the latest identified). Argentina's courier regime and tax perceptions were not researched.
- No Peru small-import (de minimis) changes or Peruvian apparel tariff changes in 2025–2026 were found.
- Central America and Caribbean tariff and regulatory specifics (CAFTA-DR origin advantages, Panama/Costa Rica de minimis) were not researched.
- Brazil's current general import tariff (TEC) on apparel and footwear, and any Mercosur TEC changes in 2025–2026, were not verified.

## 5. Which Asian/new brands are entering LATAM via licensing or distribution deals?

### Takeaway
Among the Chinese sportswear majors, only **361°** has a documented structured LATAM entry (Brazil, 2025–2026: e-commerce first, then specialist retail and planned own stores, with production shifted to Indonesia). **Anta** has only marginal cross-border and e-commerce presence in Brazil, but its ~29% stake in **Puma** (closing expected by end-2026) indirectly gives it exposure to Puma's strong LATAM business. No LATAM licensing or distribution deals were found for **Li-Ning, Xtep or Peak**. The dominant Chinese competitive force in LATAM apparel is **Shein** (third-largest apparel and footwear player in the region in 2025), with Temu and AliExpress in cross-border e-commerce. Western brand-management firms, notably **Authentic Brands Group** (Reebok and others), are expanding through local licensee networks.

It has a distribution center in Extrema (MG). ### Inferences
- Chinese sportswear brands' entry into LATAM is still early and mostly direct (own e-commerce, marketplaces, selective wholesale). That leaves room for a LATAM licensee or distributor to pitch itself as a **regional distribution partner** for 361°, Anta, Xtep, Li-Ning or Peak, especially in Mexico, Colombia, Chile and Peru, where no structured presence was found. Mexico's 35% non-FTA tariffs and Brazil's antidumping duties are a key obstacle for China-made product, which may push these brands toward Southeast Asian production (as 361° is doing) or local manufacturing partners.
- Anta's Puma stake could reshape Puma's LATAM distribution or licensing over time. This is speculative, and no source describes plans.
- Brand-management companies (ABG and others) are the most active source of available sportswear and lifestyle licenses in LATAM, licensing category by category and country by country.

### Gaps
- No LATAM distributor or licensee agreements were found for Anta, Li-Ning, Xtep, Peak or other Asian sportswear brands (e.g., Japanese Mizuno and Asics operate directly or through partners; Asics plans to quadruple LATAM stores per [Bloomberg Línea](https://www.bloomberglinea.com.br/negocios/asics-mira-crescer-em-latam-para-alem-da-corrida-e-ve-potencial-no-tenis-diz-ceo/), 2026).
- Temu's LATAM apparel share and the impact of Mexico's 33.5% courier tariff on Shein/Temu volumes in Mexico in 2026 were not quantified.
- Spanish-language trade-press coverage of Chinese sportswear brands in Mexico, Chile and Peru (e.g., Diario Financiero, El Financiero) returned nothing relevant in this round.
